Rust Telnet Honeypot

What IoT botnets do after they log in · 86 hours on Google Cloud · Nov 3–6, 2026

Rust Google Cloud Terraform nftables MITRE ATT&CK pandas Plotly
Read the full report GitHub repo
01

In short

  • What I built: a medium-interaction telnet honeypot in Rust, run on Google Cloud for 86 hours. 18 common factory-default logins get into a shell that's entirely fake. Every reply is fixed text compiled into the program, and nothing a visitor sends is ever run or fetched.
  • What I found: 481 of 2,450 connections logged in and sent 1,772 commands, including 13 attempts to download and run malware. None of them produced an outbound connection. Most bots followed the same playbook, mapped below to MITRE ATT&CK.
  • What it shows: a fixed-reply shell is safe but easy to spot. Almost every bot that checked for its own marker word left before the payload stage. Silent visitors fell from 82% to 12% after the server began opening with telnet negotiation. The IP address stayed the same, but almost none of the earlier visitors came back and the baseline is one day, so that cause isn't proven.

03

Were these IPs already known?

  • Almost all were already reported. On Nov 7, I checked all 478 IPs that visited from Nov 2 to Nov 6 against AbuseIPDB, sending only the addresses (Nov 2 was the day before the shell, when the honeypot only showed a login prompt). 472 had reports from the past year, at least 417 of them from before the honeypot first saw them, and the other 6 had none in the past year.
  • New attackers were reported fast. 3 of the 7 IPs that tried a download had no reports until after they reached the honeypot. Within three days of their first report, they had 1,055 to 5,343 each.
04

What defenders should do

  1. Block inbound telnet (23 and 2323) at the edge. 87% of connections here sent a login, and an open telnet port was all any of these bots needed to start.
  2. Change factory-default logins before a device is deployed. 18 common defaults were enough for 481 logins from 152 IPs in 86 hours.
  3. Put IoT devices on their own network segment, with outbound traffic restricted to what they need. Every dropper here had to fetch its payload from a raw IP address, over ports 80, 443, 8081 and 9087 or over TFTP. An outbound allow-list would have stopped every download in this data, as this honeypot's own firewalls did.
  4. Alert on new outbound connections from IoT devices. These devices talk to a small, fixed set of servers, so a connection to an unfamiliar address is worth a look, especially within minutes of an inbound login. The full report has a KQL query that does this from firewall logs.
  5. Use IP reputation blocklists, but as one layer, not the defense. By Nov 7, 301 of the 478 IPs (63%) scored 75 or above. On the day they arrived, fewer would have: at least 23 of those 301 had no reports until after they reached the honeypot. They include all 7 IPs behind the bots that kept coming back from one to three IPs, which score 100 now. A blocklist only knows yesterday's attackers, so it adds to steps 1 to 4 rather than replacing them.
05

The telnet opening, and its caveat

Before the shell, the honeypot only showed a login prompt, and 82% of visits sent nothing at all. 241 of those silent visits came from 11 IPs with the same rhythm: connect, stay silent for about 8 seconds, hang up and try again. Real devices start telnet negotiation as soon as a client connects, so the shell release does the same. Afterwards, silent visits fell to 12%, and 81% of visits answered the negotiation.

The caveat. Turning on the shell meant reinstalling the server, but it kept the same IP address, so a change of address doesn't explain the drop. The visitors did change: only 7 of the 117 IPs from the day before were seen again. Those 7 went from 40% to 8% silent, which points the same way, but 7 is too few to prove it, and the baseline is a single day. The 11 retry IPs never came back to the same address, so for the bots that prompted the change, it's untested. The opening is the likeliest cause of the drop, but this setup can't rule out the change in visitors.

A campaign is every shell session that sent exactly the same commands, with the probe's random tag removed.

  • New campaigns. One IP never seen before sent NUL-ended menu words (enable\0, system\0, shell\0) in 35 sessions on Nov 10, each held for the full 30 seconds. 12 sessions from 5 IPs ran the BusyBox check with a random eight-character word where earlier bots used a name such as SORA, and a new dropper, telnet2.sh, came from 3 IPs.
  • New download URLs, masked: hxxp://176[.]65[.]x[.]x/telnet2.sh, hxxp://160[.]187[.]x[.]x/all.sh, hxxp://37[.]221[.]x[.]x/all.sh, hxxp://102[.]220[.]x[.]x/curl.sh and hxxp://102[.]220[.]x[.]x/wget.sh. With one server named only for TFTP or FTP, that's 10 new addresses on 5 new servers. 6 of the 10 download sessions also tried FTP (busybox ftpget), which no session did from Nov 3 to Nov 6.
  • Outbound: still 0. The host firewall logged no blocked outbound connection and the cloud firewall's egress log had no hits, though bots sent 66 download addresses to the shell.
  • Detection fixes. The report's BusyBox rule missed a random word that starts with a lower-case letter, and its download rule missed ftpget and scripts piped out with -O- at the end of the line. Both are fixed and now tested against the real command lines, masked.
  • AbuseIPDB. On Nov 8, I reported the 49 IPs that got into the shell in the 24 hours before, and on Nov 10, 103 more that got in after that.
